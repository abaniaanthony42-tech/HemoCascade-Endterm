Central dogma | host-pathogen biology | hemoglobin

Malaria as a parasite-driven disruption of normal erythrocyte physiology

This dashboard traces the disease through the human red blood cell cytoplasm, the HBB gene, hemoglobin production, and the proteolytic pathway that turns an essential protein into a nutrient source for Plasmodium falciparum.

Species P. falciparum
Host target RBC cytoplasm
Primary nutrient Hemoglobin
Clinical burden 282M cases
Key insight

Malaria is not a host DNA mutation.

The disease is caused by parasite invasion and proteolysis of hemoglobin in the red blood cell cytoplasm.

Evidence chain

Gene → mRNA → protein → pathology

HBB makes beta-globin, which supports oxygen transport until the parasite digests it for nutrients.

Clinical impact

Fever, anemia, and severe complications

Rupture of infected cells and inflammatory responses drive the symptoms seen in severe malaria.

Knowledge Check Quiz

Section 1

Name of the Disease

"Malaria is a vector-transmitted infectious disease caused by parasitic protozoa of the genus Plasmodium, spread through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito (WHO, 2025). The name comes from the Italian mala aria, 'bad air,' reflecting the old belief that swamp air caused the disease; the actual parasite was identified in 1880 by French physician Charles Louis Alphonse Laveran, and mosquito-borne transmission was confirmed by Ronald Ross in 1897.

Of the five Plasmodium species that infect humans, P. falciparum causes the most severe disease and the overwhelming majority of deaths — about 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths worldwide in 2024, concentrated mainly in Africa (WHO, 2025). Unlike inherited disorders, malaria has no basis in the human genetic code; its pathology begins the moment the parasite enters a red blood cell and starts consuming its contents (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023)."

Section 2

Affected Organelle

"The cytoplasm of the human erythrocyte is what malaria affects. Strictly, it isn't a true organelle — it's the only compartment left after the cell sheds its nucleus and most membrane-bound organelles during maturation (Alberts et al., 2022). It's composed mainly of hemoglobin, glycolytic enzymes, and ions (Cowman et al., 2012; Molina-Franky et al., 2022).

Why it's affected: hemoglobin is the parasite's primary nutrient source (Olszewski et al., 2022; Francis et al., 1997). How it's affected: the parasite ingests host cytoplasm through a structure called the cytostome, delivers it to an acidic digestive vacuole, and breaks hemoglobin down with a cascade of proteases; most of the resulting heme is detoxified into hemozoin, but the fraction that escapes drives oxidative damage and hemolysis (Goldberg et al., 1991; Goldberg, 2020; Rocamora et al., 2021)."

Section 3

Normal Function of the Organelle

"In a healthy erythrocyte, the cytoplasm holds hemoglobin — which binds oxygen in the lungs and releases it in peripheral tissue — and the glycolytic enzymes that generate the cell's entire ATP supply, since a mature RBC has no mitochondria (Vander Jagt et al., 2020; Olszewski et al., 2022).

About ninety-five percent of the cytoplasm's protein content is hemoglobin alone (Olszewski et al., 2022) — part of why the parasite treats it as a ready-made food source."

Section 4

Gene Responsible

"Malaria has no gene of its own causing it the way adrenoleukodystrophy traces to ABCD1 — it's an infection driven by the parasite's biology, not a human genetic defect (Poespoprodjo et al., 2023). Unlike sickle cell anemia, where a single substitution in HBB (GAG→GTG) permanently changes the protein, P. falciparum causes no mutation at all; the host keeps producing entirely normal hemoglobin throughout infection (Francis et al., 1997; Goldberg et al., 2020).

HBB — the beta-globin gene on chromosome 11 (11p15.4) — is used here as the reference gene, since it encodes the protein malaria targets (GeneCards, n.d.; OMIM, n.d.). Some inherited traits do influence risk without causing the disease: sickle cell trait, for instance, measurably reduces the parasite's ability to establish itself in the cytoplasm (Fofana et al., 2023)."

Section 5

Protein Produced by the Gene

"HBB encodes beta-globin, half of adult hemoglobin (HbA): two alpha-globin chains (141 aa each, HBA1/HBA2, chromosome 16) and two beta-globin chains (146 aa each, HBB, chromosome 11), each holding one heme group for reversible oxygen binding (GeneCards, n.d.).

This is the protein 'affected' in malaria — not through mutation, but because the finished, correctly folded protein becomes the parasite's primary amino acid source once infection begins (Francis et al., 1997)."

Section 6

Symptoms

"Symptoms track the parasite's invade-digest-rupture cycle. Roughly every 48 hours, schizonts rupture synchronously, releasing merozoites and waste that trigger pyrogenic cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6), producing the fever, chills, and sweating of a malarial paroxysm (WHO, 2025).

RBC destruction outpaces replacement, causing anemia as splenic macrophages clear damaged cells alongside parasitized ones (Milner et al., 2024). In severe cases, the surface protein PfEMP1 binds host receptors (CD36, ICAM-1), causing infected cells to stick to cerebral capillaries — producing cerebral malaria, seizures, and respiratory distress elsewhere (White, 2011)."

Section 7

Cause of the Protein's Malfunction

"With no mutation involved, the cause is two-stage: getting hemoglobin out of the cytoplasm, then tearing it apart. P. falciparum forms a cytostome that pinches off vesicles of host cytoplasm, fusing them with its acidic digestive vacuole (pH 5.0–5.4) (Goldberg et al., 1991; Wiser, 2024).

Plasmepsin I makes the first cut at the alpha-globin hinge (Phe33–Leu34); falcipain-2/2′/3 cleave in parallel at multiple sites; falcilysin reduces fragments to oligopeptides; DPAP1 and aminopeptidases finish the job into free amino acids (Banerjee et al., 2002; Subramanian et al., 2009; Eggleson et al., 1999; Klemba et al., 2004). None of this touches the gene or its codons — it's entirely post-translational."

Section 8

Analysis of the DNA Sequence

"Since no mutation occurs, this works backward from hemoglobin's known amino acid sequence to its DNA (NCBI RefSeq NM_000518.5). RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3′→5′, synthesizing mRNA 5′→3′ by complementary base-pairing (A↔U, T↔A, C↔G, G↔C); the ribosome then reads that mRNA in triplets to assemble the amino acid chain shown below, with the start methionine cleaved off the mature protein (Alberts et al., 2022)."

Section 9

Mutation Analysis

"No substitution, insertion, or deletion occurs in HBB during infection, and the reason is structural: a mature erythrocyte has already expelled its nucleus and DNA before it ever meets the parasite, so there's no gene or transcript left to alter (Alberts et al., 2022; Cowman et al., 2012) — which is also why the codons and resulting amino acid sequence are unchanged. What actually changes is quantity and integrity: the protease cascade (plasmepsins, falcipains, falcilysin, DPAP1) hydrolyzes peptide bonds in an already-correct hemoglobin molecule, cutting it down to free amino acids (Banerjee et al., 2002; Eggleson et al., 1999; Klemba et al., 2004). Mutation corrupts a blueprint before a protein exists; proteolysis destroys an already-correct finished product — the effect looks similar, but the mechanism, and the correct treatment target, are entirely different."

1. INITIATION

In the nucleus, RNA polymerase binds to the promoter region of the HBB gene. Transcription begins in the 3' to 5' direction, complementing the template strand.

2. TRANSCRIPTION

Pre-mRNA is synthesized in the 5' to 3' direction, complementary to the template strand and then processed to produce mature mRNA.

3. TRANSLATION

mRNA is transported to the cytoplasm and binds to the ribosome. The ribosome reads codons and adds the corresponding amino acids to form a polypeptide chain.

4. HEMOGLOBIN PROTEIN

The polypeptide chains fold and assemble with heme groups to form functional hemoglobin in red blood cells.
